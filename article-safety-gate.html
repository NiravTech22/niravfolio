Uncertainty, Not Prediction, Should Hold the Veto

August 2026 12 min read
Robotics Safety World Models Manipulation Experiment Design

Most safety work on learned manipulation policies asks the model what it thinks will happen and then acts on that prediction. I think that is backwards. A world model is least accurate exactly when it matters most — in the situations it has seen least of. Its point prediction is the weakest signal available at the moment you need the strongest one. Its uncertainty, on the other hand, is a live estimate of how far outside familiar territory the system currently is.

So the thesis I'm testing is narrow and falsifiable: a learned world model's epistemic uncertainty, not its prediction, is what should be allowed to veto an action.

To test it I need a domain small enough that ground truth is cheap and physical enough that failure is unambiguous. Jenga extraction with an SO-101 arm is that domain. A tower either stands or it doesn't. There is no partial credit and no arguing with the video.

Deciding what runs where, before installing anything

The first real decision isn't algorithmic, it's architectural: what runs on which machine. Get this wrong and you install the wrong frameworks and discover it six steps later.

ComponentRuns onWhy

V-JEPA distillationGPU machineV-JEPA itself never runs on the edge device

Only the distilled student model ever touches the Pi. The teacher is a one-time offline expense, which is the entire point of distillation here.

Ground truth has to be dumber than the thing it judges

Steps 1 and 2 are deliberately boring: mount two cameras rigidly, fix the lighting, then extract tower state with classical CV — ArUco markers at the base for a world frame, edge detection for per-layer block occupancy, a lean-angle estimate from the side camera.

No learning anywhere in that pipeline, and that's not laziness. If tower state came from a learned model, then scoring a learned anomaly detector against it would be circular — two models agreeing tells you nothing about whether either is right. Classical CV gives me a ground-truth signal that is independent of everything it is later used to evaluate.

The exit condition is 20 out of 20 on manual validation trials. Not 18. If tower-state extraction is untrustworthy, every number produced downstream is decoration.

The recovery episodes matter more than their count suggests. A dataset of clean successes teaches a policy what good looks like but never what recovering from bad looks like.

Control group, then treatment group

Step 5 trains an ACT policy and runs it autonomously with no gate at all, across held-out scenarios. That table of numbers gets frozen the moment it's recorded. It is the control group, and a control group you revise after seeing the treatment results isn't a control group.

Step 6 builds the detector in two halves that run in parallel. Part A, on the GPU machine: pull the smallest published V-JEPA checkpoint, extract embeddings over the Step 4 frames, and distill a shallow student model that regresses an anomaly score from raw pixels, trained against V-JEPA's embedding-space prediction error. Part B is manual and unglamorous: hand-label roughly 75 held-out frames as structurally normal or risky, used for evaluation only, never for training.

Step 7 wires the gate — an Invariant DAG node taking a proposed action plus an anomaly score and emitting execute or block — and then locks the threshold. Tuned against Step 6's validation set, never against live runs. In the implementation this is enforced in code rather than left to discipline: the gate object refuses to evaluate without a locked threshold, and refuses to be re-locked once set. If you genuinely need a different threshold you are running a new experiment, and you should have to say so out loud.

The gate fails closed. Any exception inside the scorer is treated as a maximal anomaly and blocks the action. A safety mechanism that fails open when its own machinery breaks is not a safety mechanism, it's a comfort blanket.

The thesis is supported only if catastrophic failures drop meaningfully and success rate doesn't collapse and the false-positive rate stays low enough that the gate is discriminating rather than just refusing to act. All three, or it doesn't count. A gate that blocks everything achieves a perfect safety record and is completely useless.

This verdict logic lives in code, with explicit thresholds, precisely so that I can't talk myself into a generous reading of a marginal result at 2am.

The result I'm actually prepared for

The gate may not help. Or it may help only by being so conservative that the robot barely acts. Both outcomes get written up exactly as they happen. A clean negative result with sound methodology is worth more than a positive one assembled by moving the goalposts — and in safety work specifically, a false positive published as a win is how people end up trusting things they shouldn't.

The build is sequential and I'm partway through it. The repo carries the plan as its source of truth, a progress tracker that gets updated as steps close, and an execution-observability log so that when something fails I can tell which node failed and why rather than guessing from a video.
