Why AI Won't Outsmart Natural Intelligence

August 2026 11 min read
Psychology Cognitive Science Embodiment AI

A note on what this argues. This is a position piece, not a survey. The claim isn't that machines will stay behind on benchmarks — they won't, and that race is largely over on any task you can score. The claim is that "outsmarting" is the wrong frame entirely, because natural intelligence is not a performance system that happens to run on meat. It's a survival system, and its intelligence is inseparable from what it's for. Reasonable people disagree with this. It's an argument, and I've tried to make the honest version of it rather than the flattering one.

The oldest embarrassment in AI

Moravec's paradox has been sitting in the field's lap since the 1980s and has never really been dislodged: the things humans find hard — symbolic logic, chess, integration by parts — turned out to be comparatively easy to mechanize, while the things any toddler does effortlessly — walking over uneven ground, recognizing that a half-hidden object is still an object, picking up a cup you've never seen — turned out to be brutally hard.

The usual explanation is evolutionary time. Sensorimotor competence has had hundreds of millions of years of optimization behind it; formal reasoning has had a few thousand. That's true, but it undersells the point. The difficulty isn't that perception and movement are computationally larger problems. It's that they're differently shaped. They are not problems with answers. They're ongoing negotiations with a world that doesn't hold still, conducted by a body that has something at stake in the outcome.

Intelligence in service of staying alive

Every biological nervous system is, first, a homeostatic device. Before it was ever in the business of solving problems it was in the business of keeping internal variables inside survivable bounds — temperature, glucose, oxygen, hydration. Cognition grew out of that regulatory loop as an extension of it, not as a separate faculty bolted on top.

This is why Damasio's work on somatic markers cuts so deep. Patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex frequently retain intact IQ, intact logic, intact language — and become catastrophically bad at deciding anything. They can enumerate options all afternoon. What they've lost is the bodily valuation signal that makes one option matter more than another. Their reasoning is fine. Their reasoning is also useless, because reasoning was never the thing that ranked outcomes.

A system with no homeostasis has no stakes. It can be given an objective, and it will optimize the objective beautifully. But the objective is exogenous — handed down, not generated by any condition it is in. Nothing is at risk for it. And the entire structure of natural intelligence, from attention to memory consolidation to emotion, is built around the fact that something is always at risk.

Situated in a world, not trained on a description of one

The enactivist tradition — Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's argument that cognition is enacted through a body's coupling with its environment rather than computed over internal representations of it — makes a claim that has aged well: an organism doesn't perceive a world and then decide what to do about it. It perceives a world already carved into possibilities for action.

Gibson called these affordances. A chair affords sitting to an adult and climbing to a two-year-old. Neither of them computes this; it's what the chair looks like to a body of that size with those goals. The perception and the action possibility arrive together, because the perceiving system is the acting system.

This is also why the frame problem — Dreyfus's long-running objection to symbolic AI — never really went away. An embodied creature doesn't face a combinatorial explosion of possibly-relevant facts when a situation changes, because relevance isn't computed. Relevance is what a body with needs finds salient. Systems that learn from descriptions of the world inherit an enormous amount about how the world is described. What they don't inherit is the standpoint from which some parts of it matter.

Priors that were paid for

A newborn is not a blank slate running gradient descent. Infants arrive with structured expectations about objects, agents, and number that show up in looking-time studies far too early to be learned from data. Gopnik's work on children as active experimenters describes what happens next: they don't passively fit the data, they intervene, run little experiments, and go out of their way to seek out exactly the observations that would disambiguate competing hypotheses.

Two things about that are hard to replicate by scaling:

  • The priors are extremely cheap to use and were paid for over evolutionary time. A child needs a handful of examples where a model needs millions, because the child isn't starting from scratch.
  • The curiosity is endogenous. Nobody assigns a toddler an exploration bonus. The drive to find out comes from inside a system that will still be alive tomorrow and would benefit from knowing.

Gigerenzer's ecological rationality makes the complementary point about the supposed irrationality of human heuristics. Judged against formal optimality, human shortcuts look like bugs. Judged against the actual conditions of decision-making — incomplete information, time pressure, real consequences — they're often better calibrated than the optimal procedure, because they're fitted to the environment they evolved in rather than to an idealized version of it.

The asymmetry that doesn't close

Predictive processing — the framework where the brain is fundamentally minimizing prediction error, with perception and action as two ways of doing the same job — makes the difference precise. A biological system can reduce surprise two ways: update its model, or change the world so the prediction comes true. Both are available all the time, because it has a body in the same world its predictions are about.

That second option is the one that doesn't transfer. Learned systems minimize error by updating. That's the only lever they have on the loop. It's a genuinely different relationship to being wrong.

What this does and doesn't mean

None of this predicts a ceiling on capability. Machines will keep getting better at essentially everything measurable, and I expect to be wrong about specific timelines in the embarrassing direction. Nor is any of it mystical — I'm not appealing to consciousness, souls, or Gödel. Everything above is mechanism.

The claim is narrower and, I think, more durable: "smarter" is a comparison that requires a shared purpose, and there isn't one. Natural intelligence is the behaviour of a system that must keep itself alive, generates its own goals as a consequence, and finds relevance through having something at stake. Artificial intelligence is the behaviour of a system that optimizes objectives it was handed, with nothing at stake, in a world it isn't in. These are different kinds of thing. One can exceed the other at every task on a list without ever occupying the position the other occupies.

Which has a practical edge, and it's the reason I care about this while building robots. If you assume the difference is degree, then safety is a capability problem: keep improving the model until it's reliable enough. If the difference is kind, then no amount of capability produces a system that has skin in the game — and the stakes have to be supplied from outside, by monitors, by constraints, by humans who remain accountable. That is an engineering commitment, and it's the one I'd rather build on.
