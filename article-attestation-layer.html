The Attestation Layer: Autonomy's Real Blocker Isn't Capability

August 2026 9 min read
Robotics Construction Automation Runtime Monitoring Trust

Invariant started as something unglamorous: an execution-observability layer for a robot arm. Every node in a workflow emits an event — node id, stage, status, timestamp, latency, references to its input and output, and a failure reason when it fails. A flight recorder for a DAG.

The interesting part wasn't the schema. It was what the schema kept turning into every time I pointed it at a harder problem.

From logging to diagnostics

The first extension was obvious once the events existed: if you log the same DAG in simulation and on hardware, you can diff them per node. Not "sim-to-real gap" as a vague quantity, but a specific node whose latency distribution or output reference diverges from its simulated twin. That turns a research cliché into a debuggable location.

The second extension came from embedding events spatially. A robot's failures are rarely uniform across its workspace — they cluster where reach is marginal, where a camera is glare-blind, where a joint sits near the end of its calibrated range. Attaching end-effector pose to each event turns the log into a map, and the map shows you things a time series won't.

The third is where it got sharp: a proprioceptive veto. Compare what the high-level policy believes it is doing against what the low-level sensors say is actually happening, and stop when they disagree. That comparison is only meaningful if the two signals are genuinely independent. Which raises the question that ended up reorganizing the whole project.

Independent of what, exactly?

A monitor that shares the policy's cameras, the policy's clock, and the policy's learned representation will fail in the same conditions the policy fails in. Correlated failure is the default outcome of monitoring a system with a component that resembles it. You don't get a second opinion by asking the same model twice.

Meaningful independence means different sensors, different timing, and ideally a different kind of model — which is exactly why, in the Jenga safety-gate experiment, tower state comes from classical CV while the anomaly signal comes from a distilled world model. Two mechanisms with different failure surfaces.

Why this matters most in construction

Apply the same structure to construction robotics and it stops being a debugging tool and becomes an attestation layer.

The blocker on unattended autonomous work on a real job site is not that the machines can't do the task. For a growing set of bounded tasks, they can. The blocker is that nobody with liability exposure will accept the output without evidence. A general contractor, a surety, an inspector — none of them are in a position to evaluate a neural network. They need an auditable record from something that wasn't the robot's own optimistic self-report.

That reframes the monitor's job. It isn't there to make the robot safer in the abstract. It's there to make the robot's execution legible to a third party who has every reason to be skeptical. Different requirement, different design:

  • Independence is a hard requirement, not an optimization. A monitor sharing the policy's perception stack is worth roughly nothing as evidence.
  • The record must be append-only and complete, including the runs that went badly. A log you can curate after the fact is a marketing document.
  • Blocked actions are as important as executed ones. "The system declined to act 14 times and here is the state that triggered each" is the part an inspector actually cares about.
  • Diversity beats redundancy. Three copies of the same monitor fail together. Two different monitors disagree usefully.

What it looks like in practice

The event schema in the Jenga experiment is deliberately the same minimal one, applied to a domain small enough to actually finish. Six stage types, six statuses, one append-only log. Two of the statuses exist specifically for this purpose: blocked, when the gate vetoed a proposed action, and flagged, when an anomaly was noted but allowed through as advisory.

{
              "node_id": "gate_decision-4f2a9c1b",
              "stage": "gate_decision",
              "status": "blocked",
              "timestamp": 1755712043.118,
              "latency_ms": 3.7,
              "input_ref": "ep-0d41…:117",
              "output_ref": "block",
              "failure_reason": null,
              "robot_id": "so101-default",
              "spatial_pose": [0.212, -0.038, 0.094]
            }

That single record says what decided, at which stage, how long it took, on which timestep of which episode, where the arm was in space, and what it decided. Aggregate a few thousand of them and you have something you can hand to someone who doesn't trust you — which is the actual deliverable.

The unglamorous claim

I don't think the next unlock in field robotics is a better policy. I think it's the boring infrastructure that lets a policy's behaviour be audited by someone who wasn't in the room. Capability is advancing fine on its own. Accountability isn't, and accountability is what's actually gating deployment.
